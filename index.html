NSF CyberTraining: Pilot · Collaborative Research

Concurrent Computing Curricula for Cyber-Physical Systems

Learning modules and open tools that teach engineers to reason about concurrency, timing, and fault tolerance in the software that runs cars, aircraft, and robots.

Western Michigan University & Michigan Technological University August 2026 – July 2028
NSF Award 2612129 NSF Award 2612130

About the project

Automobiles, aircraft, and other safety-critical systems depend on software that performs many tasks at once. Small timing or coordination mistakes such as race conditions, deadlocks, and stale data can go unnoticed in testing and later cause unsafe behavior.

The engineers who build these systems are often trained in mechanics and electronics rather than concurrent programming. This project develops a modular curriculum and open educational tools that give them hands-on experience with concurrent and distributed computing, grounded in the platforms they use: POSIX threads, FreeRTOS, and ROS 2.

The work builds on two decades of NSF-funded concurrency education tools, ThreadMentor and ConcurrentMentor, which the team is modernizing and retargeting for engineering audiences.

Learning modules

Each module includes notes, exercises, and animations. Modules assume a first programming course in C, C++, or Python and require no prior coursework in operating systems or distributed systems.

1

Foundations of Concurrent Computing

Led by Jean Mayo, Michigan Tech

Seven 3–4 hour units that pair lecture with hands-on visualization:

  • Concurrent execution and nondeterministic interleaving
  • Shared state, race conditions, and atomicity
  • Synchronization primitives: mutexes, semaphores, monitors, channels
  • Ordering, causality, and happens-before
  • Deadlock, livelock, priority inversion, and starvation
  • Event- and message-driven execution in ROS 2
  • Testing and debugging concurrent systems
2

Fault Tolerance in Distributed Systems

Led by Ali Ebnenasir, Michigan Tech

  • Taxonomy of faults, errors, and failures; fault injection
  • Traditional methods: replicated state machines, checkpointing, rollback
  • Distributed protocols: two-phase commit, leader election, Paxos, Raft, Byzantine tolerance, specified in TLA+ and Promela
  • Modern methods: fault span, failsafe / nonmasking / masking tolerance, detectors and correctors, self-stabilization
3

Timing and Safety in Autonomous Vehicle Software

Led by Zachary Asher, Western Michigan

Perception-to-control pipelines treated as concurrent systems. In a lab that bridges ROS 2 with the CARLA simulator, students vary QoS settings such as history depth, reliability, and deadlines, then watch how delayed or dropped messages, missed deadlines, and priority inversion affect vehicle behavior.

For ME/CS 5813 Autonomous Vehicles and ME 4650 Vehicle Dynamics and Control

4

Safety and Security Assurance in Aviation Systems

Led by Radu Babiceanu, Western Michigan

Agent-based modeling and simulation in AnyLogic, NetLogo, OMNeT++, and NS-3:

  • Surveillance missions with swarms of unmanned aerial systems
  • Aeronautical telecommunication network mobility (MIPv6)
  • Collision avoidance as a system invariant, verified in Event-B

For ECE 5800 System Modeling and Simulation

Security throughout. Vulnerabilities that come from concurrency, such as timing flaws, unchecked inputs, and unsafe handling of shared resources, are taught inside the modules where they arise.

Tools

A toolchain that lets students watch what their concurrent programs actually do, without rewriting them.

Common schema, ring buffer, on-device agent

Visualization

Web and desktop views, live or replay

Wrappers and libraries

Source-compatible wrappers for POSIX threads (mutexes, semaphores, reader–writer locks, barriers, Hoare and Mesa monitors), FreeRTOS (tasks, queues, semaphores, event groups, timers), and ROS 2 executors in rclcpp and rclpy. Swap one header and link the library; program logic stays unchanged.

Built-in fuzzing inserts controlled random delays at synchronization points to surface races that ordinary runs hide.

Visualization system

Coordinated views of a single execution:

  • Timeline view of threads, tasks, and callbacks
  • Happens-before and causality view
  • Object-centric view of each lock, semaphore, or channel
  • Component grouping for ROS 2 nodes
  • Play, pause, step, scrub, and reverse

Color-blind-safe palettes, redundant shape cues, and full keyboard navigation.

CEDL

The Concurrent Event Description Language lets instructors script an exact interleaving instead of hoping a real run reproduces it. Each thread lists its events with a logical time; the interpreter replays them in the visualizer, forward or backward.

// Thread A          // Thread B
shared A;            shared A;
mutex_lock(lock,2)   mutex_lock(lock,1)
write(A,5)           read(A,3)
mutex_unlock(lock,6) mutex_unlock(lock,4)

Prototype

The prototype visualizer is being tested in CS 3331 Concurrent Computing at Michigan Tech. Clicking an event opens the source line that produced it.

Evaluation

Pre- and post-course surveys measure changes in students' understanding of and confidence with concurrency concepts. The libraries and CEDL are evaluated through user surveys, and the visualizer through formal usability testing with students and faculty.
